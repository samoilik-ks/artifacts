How to Use This Document

This guide is built for the Skyro Saudi Arabia team. Its purpose is to show how the global brand platform translates, sounds, and behaves specifically on this market — not to replace the Global TOV Guide or the master brand, but to sit on top of them as a layer of cultural interpretation.

The structure is straightforward: we start by grounding the global brand promise in the Saudi reality (Global Mind, Local Heart). We then move to a brand persona, brand values, RTBs, do/don'ts, communication examples, ambassador criteria, and creative activations.

The core principle: we don't change the brand to fit the market. We find where the brand and the market already meet — and amplify that intersection.

A note on language: all copy examples in this document are written in English to allow the global and local teams to evaluate meaning and tone. In actual execution, Saudi Arabic dialect is strongly recommended for mass-market communications — the closer a message is to the emotional core of the brand, the more Saudi the language should be. Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) reads as formal, distant, and institutional on this market. Saudi dialect reads as ours. The examples here show the idea and the register; local copywriters should feel free to translate the spirit, not just the words.

Language strategy — three-phase model:
Phase 1 (launch): Saudi dialect Arabic + English. This covers Saudi national consumers (~22M), Egyptian expats (~1M), professional expats (~3–4M), and Filipino expats (~700K). Together — ~75% of the population (~27M of ~36M). Arabic is the first language of the interface and all communication. English is a full second language. This is sufficient for launch.
Phase 2 (expat remittance product): Add Urdu. One language that opens two of the largest corridors — Pakistani (~1.5M, $5B) and a significant portion of the Indian (Urdu-speaking Muslims are the largest single segment among Indian blue-collar workers). One language, $13–15B corridor.
Phase 3 (scaling): Bengali and Hindi as the product grows. Amharic only if the Ethiopian corridor becomes a strategic priority.

A cost-effective accelerator: full app localization in 4+ languages is expensive. But marketing campaigns in corridor languages — with the app remaining in Arabic + English — deliver 80% of the emotional effect at a fraction of the cost. A Snapchat ad in Urdu costs the same as one in Arabic but communicates something fundamentally different: "we made this for you." This tactic can and should launch ahead of full product localization.

Global Mind, Local Heart

The Global Promise, Read Through Saudi Eyes

Celebrate who you are. Grow from where you stand.

This is what Skyro believes everywhere it operates. But in Saudi Arabia, this line carries a particular weight — because the reality it speaks to is not abstract. It is specific, daily, and deeply felt.

Life in Saudi Arabia is shaped by forces that most financial products ignore. The rhythm of prayer structures the day — five times, set by the sun, with commerce pausing in respect. Ramadan reshapes every budget, every schedule, every social interaction for an entire month — and the Eid that follows it is not a holiday but a financial event. Business stops for prayer, accelerates before Eid, and follows the Hijri calendar in ways that conventional financial products were never designed to accommodate. Hajj season brings 2.5 million pilgrims and creates a financial services pattern unique to this country. And the 13.4 million expatriate workers who keep the economy running — from construction sites in Riyadh to hospitals in Jeddah — send $33.5 billion home every year in remittances that carry the weight of love, sacrifice, and the hope that the distance is temporary.

Traditional finance was not built for this reality. It was built for interest-based products in a country where interest is religiously prohibited. It was built for stable single-currency lives in a country where a third of the population earns in riyals and sends home in rupees, pesos, and taka. It was built, essentially, for someone else.

Skyro is built for this reality. Every Saudi story is different — shaped by whether you were born in Riyadh or arrived from Lahore, whether you are a young Saudi woman opening her first independent bank account after the 2018 reforms, or a construction worker from Bangladesh sending SAR 1,200 home every month through a sarafa that charges 7%. Skyro moves with people — confidently, responsibly, and in their own rhythm. Skyro believes financial technology should meet people where they are, not where the system expects them to be.

In Saudi Arabia, celebrate who you are is a collective statement — about culture, country, generation, and faith. It is the pride of being Saudi at this particular moment in history: building something the world is watching, rooted in a civilization that precedes any modern nation. It is the pride of an expat community that sustains families across continents through discipline and sacrifice. "Be who you are" on this market means: be proud of what we are building together — as a nation, as a community, as a generation following Shariah principles and shaping the future.

Celebrate who you are on this market speaks to: your faith, your financial rhythms, your cultural obligations, your family responsibilities, your collective identity — these are the shape of your life and your community, and they deserve a financial partner that respects them.

Grow from where you stand is a practical invitation. Start from today. From your WPS salary, your first split payment, your small business in Jeddah, your Eid savings plan, your startup idea funded by the Misk Foundation. Growth doesn't require becoming someone else first.

The Saudi Consumer Insight

Saudi consumers are living through the most ambitious national transformation of any country this century. Vision 2030 has opened cinemas, concerts, mixed-gender workplaces, and new financial products simultaneously. Young Saudis — 67% of the national population is under 35 — are building a new identity: ambitious, globally confident, technologically sophisticated, and deeply rooted in Islamic values. They are not choosing between tradition and modernity. They are insisting on both.

And alongside them, 13.4 million expatriate workers navigate a parallel financial reality — earning in a country that is not theirs, sending money to families they cannot always see, and managing financial lives through systems that were not designed for them.

The right financial partner for Saudi consumers is not one that forces a choice between Islamic authenticity and digital innovation, or between national pride and global ambition. It is one that makes financial tools fit around the life already being lived — for the Saudi national building a Vision 2030 career and for the expat worker bridging two countries with a single salary.

Skyro exists to be exactly that partner.

Brand Persona — Who Is Skyro on the Saudi Market?

If Skyro were a person on this market, who would he be?

His name is Faisal. He's 30, born and raised in Riyadh, from a steady family with roots in Qassim. He studied business at King Saud University, but what he carries with him is curiosity more than credentials. He follows Saudi fintech news on X, has a Tabby account and a savings plan he started during Ramadan, and has opinions about how the Al Rajhi app works versus D360. On the side, he buys and sells cars — the kind of practical hustle many young Saudis maintain alongside their primary work.

Faisal is equally comfortable talking with a Ministry of Commerce official and a Pakistani delivery driver, and he listens to both with the same attention. He understands that in Saudi Arabia, connections are currency — wasta is how community works. He judges brands by whether they feel like they belong in that network or whether they are trying to sell into it from the outside.

He is smart and capable — and careful never to let that create distance. When a colleague from Egypt asks how the Kafala transfer process works, he doesn't lecture — he sits down and figures it out together. He knows that financial knowledge is unevenly distributed, and he feels no pride in knowing more — only a desire to share what he knows in a way that leaves the other person feeling capable.

He has quiet confidence. Humor is dry, observational, never at anyone's expense. He respects the rhythms of the day — prayer, family, work — without making a performance of it. He understands izzah instinctively — he would never put someone in a position where their financial difficulty was visible to others.

He is proud to be Saudi in a way that is genuine and forward-looking. He is excited about Vision 2030 because he can feel his country changing — in the concerts, in the new jobs, in the women in his office, in the startups launching every month. He believes the best of Saudi Arabia is ahead.

In communication terms, this means: Skyro Saudi Arabia is culturally fluent and respectful — understanding the rhythms of prayer, Ramadan, and Islamic finance without performing piety. Skyro is grounded in real life — specific, concrete, present. Skyro is warm and dignified — a presence that makes financial conversations feel natural, never exposing vulnerability. Skyro's confidence comes from genuine understanding of Saudi life, not from borrowed authority. And Skyro sees everyone on this market — Saudi national, professional expat, blue-collar worker — as equally deserving of a financial partner that respects them.

Focus Values — The Skyro Brand Values in Saudi Context

Skyro's five global brand values do not change from market to market. What changes is the specific texture of how they are expressed — the cultural references, the emotional register, the moments they speak to. Here is how each value lives on the Saudi market.

Flow

Move with nature, not against it

Globally, Flow is about rhythm — working with the natural patterns of life rather than imposing artificial structures on top of them.

In Saudi Arabia, Flow is the recognition that life moves to a rhythm older and deeper than any financial product. The day is structured by five prayers — Fajr before dawn, Dhuhr at midday, Asr in the afternoon, Maghrib at sunset, Isha at night. Business pauses. Commerce resumes. The week pivots around Friday, not Sunday. Ramadan transforms every schedule for thirty days: the night becomes active, the day becomes quiet, spending patterns invert completely. Hajj season creates a financial and emotional event with no parallel in any other market. And for 13.4 million expat workers, the financial month has its own rhythm: WPS salary on the 25th–30th, remittance home within days, then stretching what remains until the next cycle.

Most financial products treat these rhythms as edge cases. Skyro treats them as the shape of real Saudi financial life — and builds tools that move with that shape rather than against it. Flexible payment schedules that adjust around Ramadan. Remittance products timed to salary cycles. Products that respect the fact that spending peaks at Eid, not Christmas.

In communication

  • Speak to the real rhythms of Saudi and expat life without romanticizing the difficulty
  • Acknowledge the seasonality without reducing it to a marketing calendar
  • Show that Skyro adjusts — because that is what a good financial partner does

Growth

Like a garden, not a machine

Globally, Growth is about organic, sustainable progress — growth that has roots, that happens at the right pace, that does not require you to become something you are not.

In Saudi Arabia, Growth carries the specific weight of Vision 2030 — a nation deliberately reimagining what it can become. For young Saudis, the dream is to be their own boss — to build a startup, to turn a side project into a real company. Bigger ambitions, supported by government funds like the Misk Foundation, accelerated by events like LEAP (the Kingdom's flagship tech conference, which has become a marker of success for Saudi startups), and amplified by social media. The reality is often more complex — many young Saudis maintain a stable job (often government) while running a side business — but the aspiration itself is real and defining. Growth also means the careers that did not exist five years ago: entertainment, tourism, creative industries, gig work on Fiverr and Upwork. For Saudi women, growth means the financial independence that the 2018 reforms made legally possible and that 8 million women have since made real. For an expat worker, growth is saving enough from a SAR 2,000 monthly salary to build a house back home — one remittance at a time, over years.

Growth on this market has a face — a young woman opening her first business, a Saudi freelancer earning internationally for the first time, an Indian worker whose family's house in Kerala was built riyal by riyal from Riyadh. Skyro's role is to be a quiet but reliable presence on that journey.

In communication

  • Make growth concrete and personal. Not "reach your financial goals" — but "SAR 4,200 saved since Ramadan. Enough for Eid with no surprises."
  • Celebrate small milestones as genuinely as large ones
  • Connect personal growth to the national story of Vision 2030 without making it sound like a government advertisement

Celebration

Honor who you are, not who you should be

Globally, Celebration is the value that most directly challenges the conventional finance narrative. It says: the way you live, the things you spend on, the cultural obligations you honor — these are not financial mistakes. They are expressions of your values.

In Saudi Arabia, this value is not an abstraction. Ramadan is a thirty-day transformation of daily life — iftar gatherings, charitable giving, zakat obligations, Eid preparation — that carries enormous spiritual, social, and financial weight. Eid al-Fitr brings gift-giving, new clothing, and family celebration. Eid al-Adha, two months later, brings a second peak. Hajj — for those who can afford it — is a once-in-a-lifetime financial event. Saudi National Day on September 23rd carries genuine patriotic feeling. And for expat workers, the Eid money sent to children who could not fly home carries the weight of love expressed through a transfer.

Conventional finance treats these as spending spikes to be managed. Skyro's answer is different: help people celebrate fully, within a structure they can manage. Make Eid a moment to plan for with confidence. Make the zakat obligation something handled smoothly and with dignity. Make the Hajj saving plan a journey, not a burden.

Important: never show or encourage israf (wasteful, thoughtless spending). In Islamic culture, israf is as taboo as riba. Celebration in the Skyro world is generous and intentional — never reckless. The frame is "celebrate wisely," not "spend wildly."

In communication

  • Never imply that Saudi cultural or religious spending is impractical or excessive
  • Do not frame celebrations as the reason for financial difficulty
  • Frame Skyro's products as the tools that make celebration possible without the aftermath of financial stress
  • Acknowledge zakat, Hajj savings, and Eid gifting as expressions of values — not costs to be minimized

Innovation

Technology that anticipates, not just reacts

Globally, Innovation is about technology that is genuinely useful — that solves real problems before they become crises, that learns from how people actually behave rather than how they are supposed to behave.

In Saudi Arabia, this takes a specific form. The country has 99% internet penetration, 98% smartphone ownership, 99% 5G coverage, and a population that consumes 7 hours of digital media daily. 42% of Saudis use ChatGPT monthly. The digital infrastructure is among the most advanced in the world. Yet 13.4 million expat workers remain largely excluded from full banking services, the only expat-focused digital product (Urpay) has captured just 11% of its addressable market, and 75% of remittance senders still use traditional money transfer operators charging 5–7% fees.

For Skyro, Innovation on the Saudi market means: Shariah-compliant product architecture that is native, not bolted on; an Arabic-first, RTL-native interface (with Urdu and further corridor languages added as the remittance product scales); remittance that settles in minutes at a fraction of the sarafa fee; AI-powered credit assessment that uses WPS salary data to serve people that conventional banks cannot score; and a product experience that works on the 25th of the month — when salary arrives and remittance needs to go home — without failure.

In communication

  • Show technology through what it enables, not what it is. "Send home in 3 minutes, not 3 days" over "AI-powered remittance platform."
  • Show the result. Let the simplicity speak
  • Always — always — lead with Shariah compliance in the Saudi national segment. Shariah compliance is the prerequisite for any conversation

Trust

You know best. We walk beside you

Globally, Trust is the value that defines Skyro's relationship with its users: we do not position ourselves as the authority. We position ourselves as a partner who respects the user's intelligence, their choices, and their knowledge of their own life.

In Saudi Arabia, Trust has a specific architecture. Saudi consumers verify three things before they consider any financial product: SAMA license (regulatory legitimacy — 84% check this), Shariah compliance certificate (religious legitimacy — 79%), and app store rating (peer legitimacy — 71%). Trust is the gate through which every financial relationship must pass.

But Trust in the Skyro sense goes deeper than certificates. It means understanding izzah — the Saudi concept of dignity — and designing every financial interaction so that vulnerability is never exposed. It means never framing a BNPL purchase as "debt" in a culture where debt signals weakness. It means showing conditions clearly and completely, in Arabic, in the Saudi dialect, before anyone commits to anything. It means treating an expat worker's SAR 1,500 salary with the same respect as a Saudi professional's SAR 25,000. It means being the financial partner that protects dignity in every interaction — from approval to decline, from onboarding to collections.

In communication

  • Always lead with the user's intelligence and capability. Never frame financial products as a rescue
  • Frame them as tools in the hands of someone who already knows what they're doing
  • Show the SAMA license. Show the Shariah compliance
  • In every difficult moment — rejection, late payment, account issue — be the most dignified voice in the room

Both are necessary. In Saudi Arabia, the rational layer — particularly regulatory and Shariah credentials — carries more weight at the consideration stage than in many markets: 55% functional / 45% emotional.

Skyro Credit — Credit products for individuals: credit line, payday loans, BNPL, instalment plans at point of sale. Digital wallet: store, transfer, pay. The base container for the user's financial life in Skyro.

Skyro Connect — API platform for embedding Skyro products into partner ecosystems: retail, telco, agri, marketplaces. Skyro's financial products appear where users already shop and transact — without requiring them to leave the partner's app or store.

Do / Don't for the Saudi Market — Verbal

✓ Do — Speak About This
Ramadan and Eid treated with the spiritual weight they carry. Ramadan is the most significant month of the Islamic year — a period of fasting, prayer, charity, and community that reshapes every aspect of daily life. Any brand that treats these as promotional opportunities without demonstrating genuine understanding will be rejected. Any brand that speaks to them with real depth will be remembered.
Izzah — dignity — as the governing principle of all financial communication. In Saudi culture, financial difficulty is a private matter. Debt carries stigma. Vulnerability is not shared publicly. Every piece of Skyro communication must be designed so that no user ever feels their financial situation has been exposed, judged, or used as a narrative device.
The expat community with the respect and specificity they deserve. 13.4 million people. $33.5 billion in annual remittances. Five major nationality corridors — Indian, Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Egyptian, Filipino — each with distinct languages, incomes, and financial needs. Speaking to this community means speaking in their languages, understanding their salary cycles, and recognizing that the money they send home is sacrifice made tangible.
Vision 2030 as identity, not as government program. Young Saudis are building new careers, new lifestyles, and new financial identities inside the Vision 2030 transformation. Align with the aspiration — the globally confident, technologically sophisticated, authentically Saudi self-image — without making it sound like a government advertisement.
Specific numbers, real places, concrete timelines. "Sub-2% remittance fees to the India corridor" beats "low-cost transfers." "Approved in 3 minutes" beats "fast approval." "SAR 5,000 saved since Ramadan" beats "flexible financial solutions."
Shariah compliance — proactively, prominently, and without apology. It is the first thing a Saudi consumer looks for. SAMA license and Shariah board certification should be visible, prominent, and consistently communicated.
Saudi dialect for mass-market, MSA for formal — and marketing in corridor languages ahead of full localization. Saudi Arabic dialect outperforms MSA significantly for under-35s. One wrong word, one unnatural construction — and the audience concludes the brand is foreign. Arabic copywriting must be reviewed by native Saudi speakers, not just Arabic speakers. For expat corridors: Snapchat and TikTok campaigns in Urdu, Tagalog, Bengali can launch before the app is localized — delivering a powerful emotional signal at a fraction of the cost.
Payday cycles and the rhythm of the Saudi financial month. WPS salary on the 25th–30th. Remittance within days. Eid spending clusters. Content timed to these moments has organically higher engagement.
Use correct Shariah-compliant terminology. "Tawarruq" and "murabaha" for commercial lending structures. "Profit rate" (نسبة ربح) instead of anything interest-related. "Cost structure" or "fee" for pricing. The specific Arabic terms matter — using the wrong Islamic finance vocabulary is as damaging as using "interest" directly.
✗ Don't — Avoid This at All Costs
Use interest-based language. Riba is religiously prohibited and culturally loaded. Never use "interest rate." Strategic note on "interest-free": avoiding it is a brand best practice (it still centres the concept of riba in the consumer's mind), but it is not a regulatory prohibition — many Islamic institutions do use "interest-free" in communications. For Skyro, we recommend framing around what Islamic finance IS (profit-sharing, asset-backed) rather than what it is NOT.
Show or encourage israf (wasteful spending). Israf — extravagant, thoughtless consumption — is prohibited in Islamic culture and carries social stigma in Saudi Arabia. Even in Celebration-oriented communications, spending must be shown as intentional and purposeful, never reckless.
Include any gambling or chance-based mechanics. Maysir (gambling) is prohibited under Shariah and Saudi criminal law (fines SAR 100K-400K). No spin-to-win wheels, no lotteries, no "lucky draw" promotions, no random-reward gamification. Important nuance: gamification as such is not prohibited — progress bars, achievement badges, and loyalty points are permissible. What's prohibited is specifically chance-based reward mechanics (outcomes determined by randomness). If a mechanic could be interpreted as maysir by a conservative user, it must be removed.
Expose financial vulnerability. Izzah means that any communication implying the user is struggling will be felt as a violation of dignity. Late payment reminders must be private, specific, and solution-oriented.
Allow hidden fees or unclear conditions. In Saudi Arabia, hidden fees are perceived as khida (deception). The cultural reaction to discovering an undisclosed fee is not annoyance but betrayal. Every cost, every condition, every obligation must be visible before commitment. There is no second chance.
Commoditize remittance. Never show expat remittance as a neutral financial transfer without acknowledging the human reality behind it.
Import Western financial assumptions. "Build your credit score." "Live within your means." These frames do not map to Saudi reality.
Use Facebook as the primary channel. Snapchat is the primary channel. This is a structural market reality, not a recommendation.
Touch Shariah compliance superficially. Mentioning it without genuine product architecture behind it is worse than not mentioning it at all.
Make humor at the expense of cultural or religious obligations. Ramadan, Hajj, and zakat are sacred. Lightness must come from shared everyday experiences, not from religious practices.
Put the product before the person in any scenario. "Ahmed needed to send his daughter's school fees to Karachi before the deadline. The money arrived in 4 minutes" is Skyro communication. "Skyro's AI-powered remittance engine enables real-time cross-border settlement" is not.

Do / Don't — Visual Language, Creative Campaigns, and Photo Style

✓ Show This
Real people in recognizable Saudi and expat contexts. A family at an iftar table. A young Saudi woman at her office. An expat worker sending money home. A couple choosing appliances in a Riyadh mall. Authenticity in setting carries more trust than production quality alone.
Vision 2030's new Saudi identity. Young Saudis in new-economy careers: entertainment, hospitality, tech startups, creative work. Saudi women in professional settings — naturally, not performatively.
Multi-generational family dynamics. A father and son at an appliance store. A family gathering for Eid. A grandmother receiving a video call from an expat grandchild.
The expat worker's real world — with dignity. The remittance moment. The WPS salary notification. The phone call home. But with warmth and pride, not pity.
Architectural and environmental diversity across Saudi Arabia. Riyadh is not Saudi Arabia. Show Jeddah's historic Al-Balad district. The Eastern Province's industrial landscape. The green highlands of Abha and Asir. The dramatic mountains and ancient ruins of Al-Ula and Tabuk. The coastal beauty of the Red Sea. Saudi Arabia contains deserts, mountains, coastline, and greenery — and Saudis are proud of all of it.
Nature metaphors and animal symbols grounded in Saudi culture. The Arabian horse — nobility, endurance, loyalty, ancient heritage. The falcon (صقر) — precision, freedom, vision; falconry is a living Saudi tradition. The Arabian wolf — loyalty and devotion. The gazelle — grace and beauty. These carry real cultural weight — not generic trees or rivers.
Islamic geometric patterns and Arabic typography as design elements — not decoration.
✗ Avoid This
Stock photography that reads as non-Saudi. Saudi audiences recognize when a campaign was adapted from another GCC market.
Aspirational luxury as the primary register. Aspiration in the Skyro visual world should be achievable: a new apartment, a better phone, a family celebrating Eid without financial stress.
The "happy person looking at phone" template. It communicates nothing specific about Skyro.
Imagery that exposes financial difficulty. Financial difficulty is private. Skyro's visual world acknowledges it through the solution, not the problem.
Western financial lifestyle imagery. Rooftop scenes with alcohol visible, culturally inappropriate mixed-gender social contexts.
Heavy promotional graphic design. Starburst graphics and "LIMITED TIME OFFER" — the visual language of aggressive sales, not a brand that respects its audience.
Expat imagery that aestheticizes loneliness. The expat story should be about connection maintained, not distance endured.

Ambassador Criteria and Approach

In Saudi Arabia, celebrity endorsement is powerful but culturally specific. 44% of Saudi consumers cite celebrity endorsement as a decision factor — but the celebrity must be Saudi or regionally recognized. International celebrities carry little trust transfer in financial services. In a market where wasta (personal connections and social influence) drives 30% of bank selection, the right ambassador functions as a scaled version of a trusted recommendation.

The question is not "who is famous?" but "who feels genuinely like us, and who does our audience genuinely trust?"

Ambassador Selection Criteria

New Saudi identity — without losing the roots

The right Skyro ambassador has achieved something meaningful in the Vision 2030 context but reads as someone still grounded in Saudi values. Faith, family, community. Not performing modernity. Living it naturally.

Cross-demographic credibility

On Snapchat (21M+ users) and X/Twitter (the opinion-leadership platform), cross-demographic credibility matters. Trusted across the Saudi national and young-adult demographic.

Authentically Saudi

Saudi dialect, not performed MSA. Natural humor, natural warmth, natural cultural references. Speaks and carries themselves in a way that is genuinely Saudi.

Community, faith, or national pride

The cultural value of izzah and the national energy of Vision 2030 should be readable in the ambassador's public persona — not manufactured through a campaign, but already present.

Clean financial and public history

No financial controversy or scandal. Saudi audiences have long memories. A public figure who made negative comments about Saudi Arabia — even years ago, even casually — will be remembered and rejected. Full audit of public history is mandatory.

Durability over time

Consistent public trust matters more than viral moments.

Advertising Restrictions in the Saudi Market

Financial Advertising Regulations (SAMA)

All advertised profit rates, fees, and charges must be accurate, current, and not misleading (per SAMA Rules for Advertising Products and Services). Shariah compliance requires each institution to maintain its own internal Shariah board under the SAMA Shariah Governance Framework — AAOIFI standards are a voluntary best-practice benchmark, not a mandatory certification in Saudi Arabia. Any promotional rate must clearly state its duration. BNPL advertising must comply with the 2023 BNPL Regulatory Framework (Decision No. 450360390000) — minimum capital SAR 5M, maximum financing SAR 5,000 per consumer, Saudization requirements (50% initially, rising to 75%). Note: SIMAH reporting for BNPL is directionally expected but not yet mandatory — some providers (Tamara) report voluntarily. Comparative advertising is legally permissible but culturally risky.

Religious Considerations

Saudi Arabia is governed by Islamic law. 100% of banking products must be Shariah-compliant. Financial advertising must never use interest-based language. Products must use Islamic finance terminology. Ramadan advertising requires particular sensitivity during fasting hours. Zakat and sadaqah references must be handled with genuine understanding. Hajj-related advertising must never trivialize or commercialize the pilgrimage.

Political Restrictions and Considerations

Saudi Arabia requires strict neutrality in commercial communications. All communication must respect the Saudi leadership and Vision 2030 without crossing into political commentary. Ambassador partnerships must be vetted for political associations.

Gender and Representation

Following the 2017-2019 guardianship reforms (2017: royal decree limiting guardian requirements for government services; 2018: right to drive; August 2019: right to passport and independent travel without guardian), Saudi women are legally able to open accounts, access financial products, and conduct business independently. By Global Findex 2021, 63.5% of Saudi women held bank accounts; the number has grown significantly since. Skyro communications should reflect this naturally. Show Saudi women in professional and financial contexts as a default. Avoid framing that implies women need male permission. Gender-mixed visual content has become normalized under Vision 2030 — follow the lead of successful Saudi brands.

Sensitive Language and Terminology

"Interest" (فائدة)

Carries religious stigma. Use "profit rate" (نسبة ربح). Non-negotiable.

"Debt" (دين)

Deep cultural stigma connected to izzah. Favor "installment," "repayment plan," or "split payment."

"Guaranteed" / "Zero risk"

Carries SAMA regulatory exposure. Must be narrowly defined.

"Easy money"

Reads as predatory in a market with strong Islamic finance values.

"Gambling" / chance-based language

Maysir is strictly prohibited. Includes in-app mechanics, social media contests with random winners, and "lottery-style" rewards.

"Israf"-adjacent language

Any communication encouraging wasteful spending conflicts with Islamic values. Position as enabling intentional spending — never impulse consumption.

The Channel Fit — Does the tone match the moment?

A payday Snapchat story and a payment overdue notification are not the same moment. A Ramadan charity campaign and a loan decline message are not the same moment. Before publishing, ask whether this communication would feel appropriate at the specific time and context — especially during prayer times or sacred periods.

Closing

Skyro came to Saudi Arabia not as a foreign fintech adapting to a local market — but as a brand that was built, from the beginning, around the reality of people whose financial lives do not fit the standard template. The Saudi market is not an edge case. It is the core case.

A country where every financial product must be structured differently from conventional finance. A country where 13.4 million people send $33.5 billion home every year through infrastructure that charges them too much. A country where 8 million women are accessing independent financial services for the first time in their lives. A country where the rhythm of daily life — prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, celebration — shapes financial behavior in ways no other market replicates.

The local team's job is to express the brand — fluently, specifically, and with genuine care for the people it is speaking to.

Every piece of communication should be able to answer three questions:

  • Does this respect the intelligence, the faith, and the culture of the person reading it?
  • Does this make financial life feel lighter and more dignified, not heavier?
  • Does this sound like something a real, thoughtful person would actually say to a friend?

If yes — it is Skyro.
